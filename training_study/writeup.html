Follow-up to Looped Weight-Space Programming · November 2026

The Copy Plateau

Claude wrote a 19,936-weight looped transformer that solves every Maze-Hard maze. Trained from scratch on the same architecture, gradient descent solves none, even when every loop is supervised. We took the published weights apart to find out why, then fixed each failure in turn until a trained block solved every test maze up to 61×61.

Written with Claude Opus 5.5 in a Claude Code session · code and logs included

In short

  1. The 0% does not need a long horizon to happen. Even when we hand the block the true state at every loop and ask for only the next one, it learns to copy its input. It gets 99.3% of cells right and 0% of the cells that actually change. Full backpropagation through the loop ends in the same place.
  2. Three things slow or stop the step from being learned. At initialization, attention gets almost no gradient toward the neighbour cells, and none that tells the heads apart. The answer loss, at its usual weight, holds the model on the copy solution. Positions as lookup tables are slow to learn, and the written raw coordinates saturate attention from the first step.
  3. Remove those and the step is learnable. With the answer loss detached, learned position tables reach 66% of changing cells in 5,000 steps and 99.5% in 15,000 with extra losses. A relative-position prior reaches 90% in 5,000. With the routing given, 98–100%.
  4. A learned step still doesn't make a working loop. Run on their own output, every trained block failed: small errors in numbers nobody supervised grow geometrically. With the routing hard-coded and an outside step that rounds the state after every loop, one trained block solves 95% at the training size and 89–93% at larger sizes. Without those two aids, no trained block solved mazes. The written transformer gets by because it is exact. It is fragile along one axis: 0.1% noise on its position tables breaks about 40% of mazes.
  5. Building the rounding into the loop works, with one catch. Backpropagating through several rounded loops with a straight-through gradient diverged in all three runs we tried. Training each loop on its own (no gradient across loops) on the block's own 8-loop rollouts was stable, and at test time the model runs with nothing but its rounding layer. Hard-coded routing then solves 97% at 15×15 and 93% at 31×31. A learned relative-position bias solves 82% at 15×15, but only 3% at 31×31, because its soft attention leaks more as the grid grows. The paper's own architecture, with learned position tables, goes from 0% to 73% at the training size.
  6. Three more fixes, each aimed at an observed failure, close the gap. A local attention window stops the leak. The last 20% of failures were all mazes with ties, where the block had learned a distance shortcut instead of following the pointer. A margin penalty that counts every near-miss fixed that. The result is a block that learns its routing inside a fixed 5×5 window and solves 100% of test mazes at 15×15, 21×21, 31×31 (30/30) and 61×61 (10/10), exact at every loop, trained only on 15×15. Retrained from scratch in a single run, the recipe does the same for 3 of 3 seeds. In single-seed ablations, removing the margin penalty or the window broke it, and removing first-step oversampling only slowed it down. The paper's architecture reaches 91% by readout at its training size.

0What we set out to explain

Section 4 of the LoopedWSP post took the maze transformer Claude wrote, reset its weights to random and trained it on the same 1,000 Maze-Hard mazes. Every run solved 0%. Without normalization training diverged within 30 steps. With RMSNorm the model learned to copy the maze back, which already gets 87.4% of cells right. Two fixes aimed at the copying, a class-balanced loss and supervision of the distance wave and pointers after every loop, also gave 0% across 185 checkpoints. The authors guessed that gradient descent finds no partial credit along a chain of a hundred correct steps. Per-loop supervision should have removed that problem, since every loop then gets its own credit. So the guess doesn't explain the last result, and that result is the one we study.

We downloaded the published weights from the authors' repository and ported their generic forward pass to PyTorch. On 80 of our own mazes (11×11 to 31×31) the port gives exactly the down-left-right-up shortest path their runner gives. We read the meaning of the 35 numbers per cell off the weights: 7 for what and where, 3 for the distance mod 3, 4 for the pointer, 1 for the path flag, and 20 of scratch space. We then wrote a generator for the block's exact state after every loop and checked it against the real weights: 100 of 100 mazes matched at every loop. Every experiment below uses that architecture: one block, 4 heads of width 8, 86 ReLU neurons, no normalization unless stated.

Scale. All of this ran on a laptop CPU (Apple M1). We train on 1,000 generated 15×15 mazes instead of Maze-Hard's 30×30 and test on 100 held-out ones. Each run took minutes rather than the paper's 8 GPU-hours, and most variants ran with one seed. The point is to isolate mechanisms, not to match their compute. We supervise the written block's own state layout, including its one-loop lag of pointers and path. These are hypotheses about the paper's result, tested on a small analogue, not a reproduction of Maze-Hard.

1Reproducing the zero

Without normalization the loop is an exploding map. At random initialization each pass through the block multiplies the size of the state by about 1.4. After 50 loops the state is 108 times larger, and by loop 200 it is 1030. In our run without normalization the loss overflowed on its very first batch, around loop 105 of 106, before a single update. The paper's breakdown "within 30 steps" is this same blow-up. With RMSNorm the state grows only linearly, and a small perturbation neither grows nor shrinks. The norm makes training possible but does not make the loop stable.

With normalization the model learns nothing that matters. We trained the paper's strongest setting on 11×11 mazes: RMSNorm, backpropagation through the whole rollout, and a loss on the 8 search numbers plus the answer after every loop. After 1,500 steps it got 0% of the cells that change at any loop right, and solved 0 of 100 test mazes. Our small-scale setup reproduces their result.

2It fails in one step

Backpropagating through hundreds of loops is a common suspect for failures like this. So we removed it. In teacher-forced one-step training the block gets the exact state after loop t and must output the state after loop t+1, with no rollout and no long chain. If the trouble were credit assignment over a long chain, this would fix it.

It doesn't. After 6,000 steps the block gets 99.3% of cells right, which is exactly what copying its input scores, because only the frontier changes from one loop to the next. It gets 0% of the cells that change. Each head spreads its attention almost evenly over the 225 cells, about 0.004 per cell, which is 1/225. The block never looks at its neighbours, so it cannot know whether the wave has reached it.

Reaching the copy solution takes no long horizon: a single step lands there. That is enough to explain a 0%, but it does not rule out a second, long-horizon failure. Section 4 finds one. We never ran full backpropagation through the loop with the fixes below, so we cannot say which failure dominates under the paper's recipe.

3Three reasons the step isn't learned

3.1 Attention starts with almost no signal toward the neighbours

A cell joins the wave when one of its four neighbours is already in it. To learn that, a head must move its attention from everywhere to one specific neighbour, using only the gradient on its attention scores. We measured that gradient at initialization, averaged over the key's offset from the querying cell, on cells whose state should change. It is about 10−8, and its sign does not favour the neighbour offsets over any other offset. The values the head reads are random projections that the MLP does not use yet, so moving attention anywhere barely changes the loss.

So we gave the head a reader that is already perfect: the written block's value, output and MLP weights, with only the attention left uniform. The gradient toward the four neighbours becomes positive, but stays tiny (2–5 × 10−7). Uniform attention gives the neighbour 1/225 of the read, far below the threshold of the written neurons. A second problem also appears: all four heads get the same gradient. The wave rule is an OR over directions, so any visited neighbour helps any head equally, and nothing pushes one head to read "below" and another "left". The pointers and the path need exactly that split.

The trained models show it. The relative-position model in section 3.3 learned one head that reads down and one that reads left, but also a head spread about evenly over all four neighbours, which is enough for the OR in the wave. Its fourth head does nothing. Its wave is perfect, its pointers nearly so, and 78% of its path steps are wrong. This is a slow start, not a wall. Given enough steps, ordinary softmax attention with learned tables does get there (section 3.3).

3.2 The answer loss holds the model on copying

To isolate learning the MLP from learning to route, we replaced attention with an oracle that hands each head exactly one neighbour. Now only the MLP has to learn a small Boolean rule. With a loss on the 8 search numbers alone, it learns: 95% of changing cells right by step 1,500 at learning rate 10−3. Add the usual cross-entropy on the answer, the per-cell character readout the benchmark scores, and it stays at 0% for all 1,500 steps, at both learning rates we tried. The answer is almost entirely a copy of the input maze, and its loss is many times larger than the loss on the few frontier cells. Both losses act on the same residual numbers, so the answer loss decides what the step learns. Training the readout on a detached copy of the state removes the conflict. We do that from here on. We tried only weights 0 and 1 for the answer loss, so this may be a loss-balancing problem rather than anything specific to the readout. A much smaller weight might work too.

3.3 Positions stored as tables hide "the cell below"

The written block finds its neighbour with arithmetic on raw coordinates. Each cell carries r, c and r²+c², and a query−key product equal to −|pj − (pi + offset)|², times a large constant, peaks at the neighbour. Retrained from scratch, the embedding is two lookup tables, one per row index and one per column index, with nothing that says row 7 comes after row 6. We compared four ways of giving the block positions, all with teacher forcing, the answer loss detached and 5,000 steps at learning rate 2·10−3:

Learned tables do start to escape the plateau once the answer loss is detached: 66% of changing cells right after 5,000 steps, up from 0% at step 1,000. A learnable relative-position bias, one number per head per offset within two cells, reaches 90%, with two heads that clearly read one direction each. The written coordinates the solution itself uses do worst. Fed to random weights at their written scale, the written coordinates start attention in the saturated regime, where the gradient is zero. That may be a scaling problem: we did not try the same coordinates normalized. With more steps and two extra losses (section 4), learned tables reach 99.5% of changing cells by 15,000 steps (R10). So learned positions are slow, not impossible. Learning the step, though, is not the same as a working loop. Neither the relative-bias nor the learned-table blocks produce usable rollouts, even with rounding (28% and 0% at 15×15).

4A learned step is still not a working loop

With routing given, changing cells weighted up and the answer loss detached, the block learns the step: 100% of changing cells right when fed true states. Yet it solves 0 of 100 mazes on its own output. The error breakdown and a trace of one free rollout show why.

The rarest event is learned last: the path moving one cell forward. It needs "my neighbour is on the path and points at me", and there is one such cell per loop against roughly 200 that stay put. Weighting changing cells up fixes it, but that trades misses for false alarms: about 8 in 100,000 cells that should stay the same now flip.

The bigger problem is in the numbers we never supervised. We supervised 8 of the 35 numbers per cell. The block writes small changes into the other 27, the static "what and where" numbers and the scratch space, and teacher forcing never shows it the result, because every training input is clean. Run on its own output, those changes compound. The static numbers are off by 0.5 after one loop, by 1,000 after 10 and by 1031 after 60, and they take the search state with them. Adding losses that ask the block to keep the static numbers and clear its scratch, and training on inputs with noise, slows the drift but does not stop it.

A block trained by regression always leaves a little error in its output. Iterating it amplifies whatever part of that error points off the states it was trained on. The written block has no such error: on clean 0/1 inputs every output is exact. Round the search numbers to 0/1 and reset the other 27 after every loop, and the same trained weights become a working algorithm:

5The written solution is exact, and fragile in one place

Its numbers sit far from where training starts. Its row and column tables hold values up to 16,129 (127²), against an initialization scale of 0.02. Its query weights reach 566, its attention logits reach 42,000 on a 15×15 grid, and each head prefers its neighbour over the runner-up by a gap of exactly 100. The softmax derivative at that point is 3.7 × 10−44, so the attention gets no gradient at the solution at all. That much sharpness is not required, though. Scaling the query weights down, a gap of 5 still solves every maze, and a gap of 3 solves none.

It is also exact rather than robust. Change one cell's distance mod 3 at loop 6 and 55% of mazes come out wrong. Set one stray path flag and all of them do. Add Gaussian noise with σ = 0.05 to the search numbers once and no maze survives. Pointer errors are the exception: pointers are recomputed every loop, so a wrong one heals. The written block does not pull a noisy state back to 0/1. It simply never makes an error. A trained block would need the same property: across the roughly 105 cell-updates of a 15×15 rollout, and about 1.6 × 106 at 30×30, every update must be right.

6Putting the rounding inside the loop

Section 4 used rounding as an outside fix. Here it becomes part of the architecture. After every pass through the block, a fixed layer rounds the 8 search numbers to 0 or 1 and resets the 7 static numbers and the 20 scratch numbers to their embedding values. It does this at loop 0 too, so the block only ever sees clean states and the only way to go wrong is a wrong bit. Losses use the values before rounding, so rounding never hides an error, with changing cells weighted up as before. The readout trains on a detached copy of the state. At test time the model runs as it is, from its own loop-0 state, with nothing else reset or hard-coded beyond the routing variant being tested.

6.1 Straight-through gradients blow up

The standard way to train through rounding is the straight-through estimator: round on the way forward, pretend the rounding is the identity on the way back. It diverged in all three runs we tried (one seed each, learning rate 2·10−3):

  • Backpropagating through the whole rollout, with hard-coded routing, gave a non-finite loss at step 175. This run's log survives but its settings file does not.
  • Truncated to the last 16 loops, 358 of the first 500 updates had an infinite gradient and were skipped. The loss sat near 90, meaning values before rounding had moved far from 0 and 1.
  • With 8-loop rollouts from true states and a relative-position bias, the loss held near 0.2 until step 1,500, then jumped to 7.7 × 105 by step 1,650. The same run with the gradient stopped at the rounding (E2 below) trained to 82%.

Rounding makes the forward pass clean. The straight-through backward pass ignores the rounding, so it follows the unrounded dynamics, which section 4 showed are unstable. The forward pass is stabilized but the gradient isn't. Strictly, our comparison is gradient across loops against none at all: with the gradient stopped, no gradient crosses from one loop to the next.

6.2 Stop the gradient, train on your own rollouts

The fix is simpler. Stop the gradient at the rounding, so each loop is trained as a single step, but start each step from the block's own rounded output. Each training example starts from the true state at a random loop and runs 8 loops on the model's own output, with a loss after every loop. Because the rounding keeps every state clean, the only thing that differs from the training data is wrong bits, which is exactly what the block now sees and learns to correct.

All of this is dense imitation: every loop is supervised with the written block's exact 8-bit state, including its fixed tie-break and its one-loop lag, and the rounding layer resets the other 27 numbers. The block learns to reproduce a known step of a known algorithm. It does not discover the algorithm. For the paper's own architecture this is the first non-zero result: 73% of mazes solved at the training size, against 0% in every earlier setup. Its wave and pointers are now essentially right. Its remaining errors are about 2,200 stray path flags among 1.2 million cells, which mostly heal: none of its mazes is exact at every loop, yet 73% end right. It cannot go to 21×21, because table rows past 14 were never trained.

6.3 Why the relative bias stops at 21×21

E2 learned the step almost perfectly at 15×15. Teacher-forced, it gets every changing cell right, and 31 of 50 rollouts never make a single mistake. Yet it solves 1 of 30 mazes at 31×31. The reason is in how much attention leaks. Its learned biases favour neighbours by only a few logits (about +2 to +4, against −2.5 for far cells). Every cell beyond the window still gets a little attention, and there are four times as many of them on a 31×31 grid as on a 15×15 one:

Some of this is built into the parametrization: one shared bias for every far offset means far mass grows with the number of far cells at any finite bias. We have also not separated the two neighbour-reading heads from the two near-global ones as the cause. A rule that works at every size needs a logit gap that grows past log(number of cells), or attention that cannot reach far cells at all. The written block takes the first route, with a gap of 100. Section 7 tests the second.

7Pushing further

Each step below came from looking at which test mazes still failed and why. Every model is the free-running block with the rounding layer, trained as in section 6.2. F1 and F2 train from scratch for 8,000 steps. Every later run continues from an earlier checkpoint for 3,000 steps.

7.1 A local window fixes the leak

We masked attention to the 5×5 window around each cell, so no attention can reach far cells at all. The block learns as fast as before (80% at 15×15 by step 3,000), and size transfer comes back: 80% at 15×15, 69% at 21×21 and 63% (19/30) at 31×31, against 3% without the window. Training on a mix of 11×11, 15×15 and 21×21 mazes gave exactly the same numbers. With no absolute positions and a hard window, the block is close to a translation-equivariant 5×5 convolution whose weights depend on content, so good size transfer is largely built in. What it still has to learn is which offsets matter and what to do with them. For the learned position tables we tried the opposite cure: shifting every training maze to a random place in the 128×128 coordinate frame, so all table rows get trained. It destroyed what the block had learned, with 4,922 of 4,929 new pointers wrong, and did not recover in 8,000 steps.

7.2 The 80% ceiling is the ties

F1 and F2 both sat at exactly 80% at 15×15 from step 3,000 to 8,000, and E2 hovered at 80–82%. That pointed to the mazes rather than the models. In F1 19 of the 20 failures first went wrong at loop d+1, the first step of the path out of the start cell. That step happens once per maze, at a single loop, so 8-loop windows starting at random loops rarely see it. Oversampling those loops (half of all windows start just before it) moved the first error later, but the ceiling stayed. The same 20 mazes failed, and every failure was now a stray path flag off the path.

All 20 of the remaining failing mazes have a tie on their path: a path cell with two neighbours that are both one step closer to the goal. None of the 80 solved mazes does. In every failure the stray flag lands on the losing branch of the tie. The blocks had learned the shortcut "my path neighbour is one step farther from the goal than me" in place of "my path neighbour points at me". The two rules differ only at ties, and only 17% of training mazes have one on their path. The windowed models' plateaus match the tie rate of each test set exactly: 20 of 100 test mazes at 15×15 have a tie (80% solved), 31 of 100 at 21×21 (69%), and 11 of 30 at 31×31 (19/30). In the failures, the final path flags mark the union of all shortest paths.

7.3 Make every near-miss count

The loss averaged each cell's error over the grid, so one wrong bit next to a tie cost almost nothing. We added a margin term, summed rather than averaged, on any bit whose value before rounding lands within 0.25 of the threshold. That one change finished the job:

Without the window, the margin penalty alone (H2) solves 99% at 15×15 but only 1 of 30 at 31×31, so the window is what carries size transfer. Like the written transformer, it needs only more loops for a bigger maze. Unlike it, every weight came from gradient descent on 15×15 mazes. The block knows nothing about absolute position; the relative bias and the window are the only positional information it gets.

7.4 The paper's own architecture

The same recipe applied to the unmodified architecture (softmax attention, learned row and column tables, no window) went from 73% to 79% with first-step oversampling and to 91% with the margin penalty, scored on the readout at 15×15. Its path-flag state scores 0%, but that is almost all one cell. It never sets the path flag on the goal cell, which the readout ignores. In 91 of the 100 test mazes that is its only wrong cell, exactly the 91 its readout solves. It still cannot leave the training size, because its position tables have no entries trained past row 14.

How to read these numbers. Each row is one run with seed 0. H1 is the end of a warm-start chain, F1 → G1 → H1. Section 7.5 retrains the recipe from scratch on three seeds and ablates each ingredient. Each fix was chosen after looking at which held-out test mazes failed. That makes the test set partly a development set. The 31×31 and 61×61 mazes were never used for any choice, but they are only 30 and 10 mazes. The local window is a strong hand-written prior, though a generic one: it says "only look nearby", not "look down, left, right, up".

The ablations are one run each, so they show what broke in that run, not what is necessary in general:

  • Without the margin penalty the block fails exactly the mazes whose path passes a tie, and only those, at every size: 80%, 69% and 19/30 are the shares of tie-free mazes. We did not try other ways of handling ties from scratch.
  • Without the window it is still perfect at the training size, but falls to 89% at 21×21 and 0/30 at 31×31. The window is what carries size transfer, not what makes the task learnable. It is a hand-chosen, convolution-like prior that matches the one-step neighbourhood the algorithm needs, so much of the transfer is built in rather than learned.
  • Without first-step oversampling it ends in the same place, but learns more slowly: at step 2,000, 62% of 15×15 mazes were exact at every loop, against 98% with it.

The smallest margin before rounding on the 31×31 rollouts is 0.12 to 0.15, below the 0.25 the penalty aims for, and 30/30 cannot rule out a failure rate of a few percent on larger or harder mazes. The loop count of each rollout is set from the true start-to-goal distance, as in the rest of this post. The written transformer uses twice the number of cells instead, and the trained blocks stay fixed after the path is complete, so a larger budget would give the same answers.

So the whole recipe is short. It is still dense imitation: every loop is supervised with the written block's 8-bit state, starting from true states, and "from scratch" means from random weights, not without that supervision. It has five parts: a rounding layer in the loop; each loop trained on the block's own 8-loop rollouts from true states, with no gradient across loops; a relative-position bias inside a 5×5 window, with no absolute positions; changing cells weighted up; and a margin penalty summed over near-threshold bits. With it, the same one-block, 35-number, 4-head, 86-neuron architecture that solved 0% in section 1 learns a step that runs exactly at four times its training width.

8What this says about training looped models

  • Check one step before blaming the loop. Teacher-forced one-step accuracy, scored only on the cells that change, separated the problems here at once. A model that copies its input looks 99% right on every per-cell metric.
  • Keep the answer loss away from the state. When the readout mostly copies the input, its gradient sets what the step learns. Train it on a detached state, or weight it far below the state loss.
  • Give attention a relative-position prior. Lookup tables start uniform, and huge raw coordinates start saturated. A relative bias per offset learned faster: 90% of changing cells at 5,000 steps, against 66% for learned tables in the same setup.
  • Supervise the whole state, or reset what you don't supervise. Unsupervised numbers are where free rollouts break. The written block treats all 35 numbers as part of its contract.
  • Put a discretizer in the loop, and stop the gradient there. A rounding layer inside the loop took every routing variant from 0% to working loops. Straight-through gradients through that layer blew up. Stopping them, and training on the block's own short rollouts from true states, worked. It may also bear on why some trained looped models overthink and others don't.
  • Look at which examples fail, not at the average. Two of the biggest gains here came from reading failures one by one. The first path step and the ties are each rare, and each capped accuracy at a fixed share of mazes.
  • Sum the loss over rare decisions instead of averaging it away. A margin penalty summed over near-threshold bits did more than oversampling the rare events themselves (100% against 87%). Trained from scratch, it was the one ingredient that took accuracy past the share of tie-free mazes.
  • Make attention sharp, or local, if the rule must hold at every size. Soft attention leaks a share of its weight to every far cell, so that share grows with the input. A learned bias of a few logits worked at the training size and broke at twice the width.
  • Rescale the written weights before fine-tuning from them. At the solution the attention gradient is exactly zero, and 0.1% noise on the positions breaks mazes. A version with gentler scales, such as relative positions and a margin of 5 instead of 100, would probably make a better start for the paper's proposed fine-tuning experiment. We have not run it.

Back to the paper's question. The architecture can represent the solution, and the obstacle is finding it. In our small analogue there were two obstacles, and they compound. The first is learning one step: its signal starts tiny and does not tell the heads apart, and an answer loss at full weight rewards copying. The second is running the step: nothing in a trained block keeps small errors small. The paper's 0% needs only the first. Fixing the first exposes the second.

Limitations

We ran 11×11 and 15×15 mazes from our own generator, not Maze-Hard, on a laptop. Every training configuration ran once, with seed 0 and 1,500 to 15,000 steps, and learning rates differ between arms (10−3, 2·10−3, 5·10−4 for the warm start). Differences between arms are suggestive, and "never learns" means "did not learn within this budget". The section 3.2 curves are training-batch accuracies. The oracle routing, the rounding step and the true loop-0 state used in rounded rollouts are interventions, not learned behaviour: they show what the remaining problems are, not that gradient descent solves them. Part 6 tried straight-through rounding (it failed) and gradient-stopped training on own rollouts (it worked). Section 7.5 ran the final recipe from scratch on three seeds, but each ablation ran on one seed. We did not try full backpropagation through the loop combined with the fixes of sections 3 and 4, normalized written coordinates, intermediate answer-loss weights, fine-tuning from the written weights, or the paper's curriculum.
